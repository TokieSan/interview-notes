# Behavioural Interview Frameworks

Structure, rubric decoding, story-writing rules and method answers. Pair with [question-bank.md](question-bank.md), [story-template.md](story-template.md) and [story-rubric-prompt.md](story-rubric-prompt.md).

# Answer structure

**STAR** — the universal structure for behavioral answers:
- **S**ituation — context, 1–2 sentences. *Where, when, what was at stake.*
- **T**ask — your specific responsibility / the problem you owned.
- **A**ction — what **YOU** did (60% of the answer; use "I," not "we"). The decisions, the *why*.
- **R**esult — the outcome, **quantified**, plus what you learned.

**SPSIL** (Google-favored expansion — adds reflection):
- **S**ituation → **P**roblem → **S**olution → **I**mpact → **L**earning.
The extra **L**earning beat is what signals humility + growth mindset (core Googleyness). Always end with "what I took from it."

**Timing:** 2–3 minutes. Practice with a timer. If you're past 3 min you're rambling.

**The "I vs we" rule:** describe the team for context, then switch hard to "I" for your actions. Interviewers are grading *you*, and an answer drowning in "we" reads as someone hiding a small contribution.

---

### Formats by company

**Google — SAIL.** Situation (15s, with stakes) → Action (45s, first person singular) → Impact (20s, quantified) → Learning (10s). Cut Task entirely; fold the framing into Situation's last clause.

**Apple — SAIL + P.** Same, plus **Process change** — what you changed so it can't recur. Apple asks this directly after incident stories and treats "we added monitoring" as a non-answer. The strongest version has layers — e.g. a technical fix *plus* a monitoring change *plus* an organisational policy change; the policy one impresses most because it's not technical.

**Length discipline.** Every story needs a 90-second version. The long version is for "walk me through it"; the 90-second version is for everything else, and it's the one you'll actually need mid-technical-round.

# What's being graded

Two attributes, scored by every interviewer:

**Leadership = emergent, not title-based.** "They lead and influence effectively, manage projects, get things done, work as a team, and strive for self-development." → Show you move things forward and bring people with you *without* needing authority.

**Googleyness = 8 traits** (synthesized from Google's own sources):
1. Comfortable with ambiguity
2. Humble, values feedback ("intellectual humility")
3. Bias for action
4. Does the right thing
5. Takes ownership ("owners, not employees")
6. High standards / dreams big ("great just isn't good enough")
7. Thinks freely / challenges status quo
8. Enjoys fun (collaborative, not a tyrant)

The three the recruiters name most in official videos: **ambiguity, collaboration, bias to action.** Weight your stories toward those.

**"Culture add," not "culture fit."** They want what you'd *add*. Lean into whatever is distinctive about your path — those are assets.

**Background reading if you have an evening:** Google's "Ten things we know to be true" (about.google/philosophy) and the idea behind Laszlo Bock's *Work Rules!*. Skim, don't cram.

# Why stories fail, and what strong ones have

Most behavioral questions are the **same competency in different words.** "Tell me about a time you took ownership," "your biggest accomplishment," and "a project you drove start to finish" all want the *same* story. So: **build ~8 strong, flexible stories — one per cluster below — and you can answer almost anything.** A great story usually flexes across 2–3 clusters by shifting emphasis, so 8 stories over-covers a 45-min round (4–6 questions + follow-ups).

**Pick ONE real experience per cluster.** If you have something more recent, more concrete, or that you can talk about with more energy, use that. The stories that land are the ones you actually lived and remember in detail.

---

### Why stories fail

A behavioral story dies when it has any of these. Audit every story you write against this list:
- **Vague situation** — no stakes, no scale, no "why this mattered." (Fix: one sharp sentence with a real number or real consequence.)
- **"We" instead of "I"** — the grader can't find *your* contribution. (Fix: team for context, then hard switch to "I did / I decided.")
- **No clear action** — jumps from problem to result with no decisions shown. (Fix: 60% of the story is the specific things *you* did and *why*.)
- **No quantified impact** — "it went well." (Fix: a number, a before/after, or a concrete outcome.)
- **No reflection** — no "what I learned / would do differently." (Fix: one honest closing beat.)
- **Can't survive a follow-up** — falls apart at "what was the hardest part / how did others react / what would you change." (Fix: pick a story with real depth, not a tidy fairy tale.)
- **Too long / rambling** — 5 minutes of preamble. (Fix: 2–3 minutes, action-dense.)
- **Generic** — could be anyone's story. (Fix: specific names, systems, decisions only you would have.)

### What a STRONG story has (write toward this)
1. **Stakes in one line** (scale/consequence/why it mattered).
2. **A clear personal mandate** — what *you* owned.
3. **2–3 concrete decisions** with the *reasoning* behind them (this is the signal).
4. **A tension** — something was hard, ambiguous, or contested (no tension = no signal).
5. **A quantified result.**
6. **A reflection beat** — what you learned / would do differently.
7. **Depth to spare** — more detail than you'll use, so follow-ups are easy.

---

### Editing rules

- **Task sections that contain Actions.** Collapse to one sentence or cut.
- **Italicised parentheticals for the good bits.** Strong material often hides in the margins — promote it.
- **Share metrics without counts.** "80% of pre-prod defects" invites the obvious probe ("80% of how many?"). Lead with the absolute count.
- **Telling the same project four times in one loop.** Vary deliberately.
- **Filling the silence after you finish.** 90 seconds, then stop. Their turn.

# Hypothetical / situational questions ("Imagine you…")

These test *judgment + structure*, not a memorized story. **Framework for ALL of them:** (1) clarify the goal & constraints, (2) gather info / diagnose root cause, (3) involve stakeholders, (4) act with empathy + data, (5) define what success looks like, (6) follow up. Show empathy AND results.

#### Q. A teammate consistently misses deadlines. What do you do?
1:1 first, privately — **assume good intent, diagnose root cause** (blocked? overloaded? unclear scope? personal issue?). Address *that*, not the symptom. Offer help / re-scope / unblock. Escalate only if it persists and affects the team. **Lead with empathy, end with the project still succeeding.**

#### Q. Your team faces a problem nobody has expertise in. How do you proceed?
Acknowledge the gap openly. Break the unknown into smaller knowable pieces. Timebox spikes to reduce uncertainty. Pull in expertise (other teams, docs, prototypes). Bias to a small experiment over endless analysis. Share findings so the whole team levels up.

#### Q. How would you assign roles starting a new project?
Map work to strengths + growth goals (stretch people deliberately). Clarify ownership and interfaces up front. Lightweight process (who decides what). Leave slack for the unknown.

#### Q. You join a team with a negative/toxic culture. How do you improve it?
Listen first (don't barge in judging). Find root causes (burnout? no recognition? unclear goals? bad incident hygiene?). Model the behavior you want (blameless postmortems, crediting others, psychological safety). Small concrete wins over grand pronouncements. Measure morale change.

#### Q. How would you improve inclusion on your team?
Concrete actions, not slogans: ensure quieter voices are heard in design reviews (round-robin, written input first), inclusive on-call/meeting scheduling across time zones, mentorship, fair credit. Tie to "culture add" — diverse approaches make systems more robust.

#### Q. Imagine you're organizing a large event/launch [the official guide's example]. Walk me through it.
Define the objective + success metric → audience/stakeholders → logistics (venue, time, setup) → owners for each workstream → risks + contingency → measure outcome.

#### Q. How do you prioritize when everything is on fire (multiple incidents/competing asks)?
Triage by impact (user-facing/SLO-breaching first), stop the bleeding before root-causing, communicate status, delegate, defer non-urgent. **SRE framing:** protect the error budget; mitigate then investigate.

# Method answers to have ready (Apple asks for these directly)

### "How do you make decisions?"

> "I try to make the decision reversible or cheap first. So: what's the smallest thing that tests the risky assumption? Then I write down the constraint set — what's fixed, what's negotiable — and the two or three options with what each costs. I pick based on which failure I'd rather have, because there's usually no option without one. Then I state the decision, the reasoning, and what would make me reverse it, in writing, so the team can disagree with the reasoning rather than the conclusion. For irreversible decisions I spend much longer and get more people in the room; for reversible ones I optimise for speed of learning."

### "How do you define scope?"

> "I start by writing down what problem we're solving and for whom, because scope arguments are usually disguised disagreements about the goal. Then I split it three ways: what must be true for this to be worth doing at all, what's valuable but severable, and what's explicitly out — and I write the *out* list down, because unwritten exclusions come back. I try to find the smallest version that produces a real signal, and I timebox to that first. Then I name the dependencies and who owns each, and I agree a checkpoint where we decide to continue, cut, or change shape — before we've sunk the effort, so the decision is cheap."

**Then the communication/direction half:**

> "Written first, verbally second. A short design doc — problem, constraints, options, decision, open questions — circulated before the meeting, so the meeting is for disagreement rather than transmission. I try to make the reasoning visible so people can attack the reasoning. For direction, I'd rather give someone the constraint and the goal than the implementation, and be available; and I flag risks early and loudly even when they're not mine, because the cost of an early false alarm is much lower than a late true one."

### Telling a production-outage story

Apple (and many SRE loops) ask *"how have you handled a production incident in a system you owned?"* and treat "I've never had one" as disqualifying inexperience. Candidates who describe an incident but wrote no postmortem get asked why. Structure it like a system design answer:

1. **The system** (30s): what it did, its scale, what depended on it.
2. **The symptom**: what users/consumers actually experienced, quantified.
3. **Detection**: alert, user report, or your own monitoring? *Be honest — "a consumer told us" is a legitimate answer if you then closed that gap.*
4. **Triage and blast radius**: how you scoped it, what you ruled out and how.
5. **Mitigation**: what you did to stop the bleeding, and how long it took.
6. **Root cause**: plural. What the actual mechanism was.
7. **Permanent fix**.
8. **The postmortem and the process change**: *"what prevented recurrence?"* — they will ask this.
9. **The detection gap**: what you changed in monitoring so the next one is caught earlier.

Multi-cause stories beat single-cause ones — single-cause stories are usually incomplete stories.

### "Why us?"

Generic answers ("great products", "impact at scale") register as unprepared. Name a **specific technical decision or practice you respect** at the company, and connect it to something you've actually done. Make it something only you would say. Prepare ~45 seconds; don't improvise.

# Red flags (instant-deduct behaviours)

- ❌ Blaming a former employer, ex-managers, or teammates for anything (including a layoff).
- ❌ Vague answers with no concrete "I did X." Use **I**, not "we," when describing *your* contribution.
- ❌ No measurable result. Always close with the outcome/impact.
- ❌ Rambling >3 min with no structure. Use STAR.
- ❌ Claiming you never fail / never get feedback / never disagree. Reads as low self-awareness.
- ❌ Generic "Google is a great company." Be specific about SRE + scale + the craft.
- ❌ Defensiveness on follow-ups. They probe to see if it's *your* story — welcome it.

## The one-line mantra
**Empathy + ownership + data + a measurable result.** Hit those four in every answer and you're Googley.

**Leaving a job after a layoff?** State it plainly and briefly ("my role was affected by a broader org reduction"), then pivot to what you're looking for next. No blame, no over-explaining, no emotion.
